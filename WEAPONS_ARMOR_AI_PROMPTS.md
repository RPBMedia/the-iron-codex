# AI illustration prompts — Weapons & Armor (8 remaining subjects)

Written 2026-09-06, after every photographic source was exhausted: Wikimedia
Commons (categories, `intitle:`, full-text), the en-wiki articles' own image
lists, Openverse (Flickr + Wikimedia + museums), the Met and Cleveland CC0 APIs,
and the maker/retailer sites Wulflund, Museum Replicas, Cold Steel and Kult of
Athena.

Seven of the eight fail for one reason: **polearms are shipped and photographed
as heads**, by museums and makers alike, because a two-metre shaft fits neither a
studio frame nor a parcel. Wulflund states it outright ("delivered without wooden
pole for cheaper and easier shipping").

**These are last-resort placeholders.** Per `CLAUDE.md`, a real photograph always
takes precedence: if one becomes sourceable later, replace the AI image and drop
the `aiGenerated` flag.

---

## Rules that apply to every prompt below

Append this block to each subject prompt.

> Photographed as a museum catalogue product shot: the **complete object isolated
> on a plain seamless white background**, even soft studio lighting, no shadows
> beyond a faint contact shadow, no scene, no setting, no figure, no hands, no
> props, no text, no watermark, no border. The whole object must be inside the
> frame with a small margin — nothing cropped at any edge. Natural proportions,
> no perspective distortion, photorealistic, sharp focus throughout.
>
> **Historical scope: Europe, c. 1000–1500 only.** Not Viking-fantasy, not
> Roman or Iron Age, not 16th–17th century, not Japanese, Ottoman or
> non-European. No fantasy or game-inspired design.
>
> **Materials and finish:** bright polished or lightly satin steel with visible
> forging texture — **never blackened, blued, chemically antiqued or
> "battle-worn"**. Wood is plain ash or oak with a natural oiled finish.
>
> **No decoration of any kind**: no engraving, etching, knotwork, runes,
> inscriptions, gilding, jewels, leather wrapping in decorative patterns, or
> maker's marks. These were working weapons; the plain surface is the accurate one.

## 1. `spear` — European winged spear

> A medieval European winged spear (Flügellanze), c. 1000–1200, shown complete
> and horizontal. A leaf-shaped double-edged steel blade about 30 cm long on a
> conical socket, with two short horizontal lugs (wings) projecting sideways
> where the blade meets the socket. The socket is riveted to a straight ash shaft
> about 2 metres long, plain and untapered, with a simple iron ferrule at the
> butt.

Frame: wide landscape, weapon laid horizontally corner to corner.
Avoid: barbed or harpoon-like heads; spiral or twisted shafts; leather grip wraps.

## 2. `bill` — English bill

> An English infantry bill, 15th century, shown complete and vertical. The head
> is a broad curved blade with a concave cutting edge, derived from an
> agricultural billhook, carrying a forward-pointing spike at the top and a
> smaller rear hook or spike behind. The head is socketed onto a straight ash
> haft about 2 metres long, with two iron langets running down the shaft from the
> head and riveted to it.

Frame: tall portrait, weapon vertical, head at top.
Avoid: symmetrical axe blades (that is a halberd); glaive-like single blades.

## 3. `poleaxe` — knightly poleaxe

> A knightly poleaxe, c. 1400–1480, shown complete and vertical. The head has a
> crescent axe blade on one side, a squared hammer face with small pyramidal
> spikes opposite it, and a long spike on top. Below the head, iron langets run
> down a straight ash haft about 1.7 metres long. A plain iron rondel disc guards
> the hands below the head, and the butt is capped with an iron ferrule.

Frame: tall portrait, weapon vertical, head at top.
Avoid: double-bitted heads; ornate flanges; hammer faces shaped like modern tools.

## 4. `halberd` — Swiss/German halberd

> A Swiss or south German halberd, 15th century, shown complete and vertical. The
> head combines three elements forged as one: a broad axe blade with a slightly
> concave edge, a pointed rear fluke opposite it, and a long straight spike rising
> above both. The head is socketed with iron langets riveted down a straight ash
> haft about 2.2 metres long.

Frame: tall portrait, weapon vertical, head at top.
Avoid: the elaborate pierced and etched heads of ceremonial 16th–17th-century
halberds — this is a plain fighting weapon.

## 5. `lance` — cavalry lance

> A medieval European cavalry lance, c. 1300–1450, shown complete and horizontal.
> A long ash shaft roughly 3.5 metres long, thickest at the grip and tapering
> gently towards both ends, fitted with a small narrow steel head of lozenge
> section. A plain conical steel vamplate — a hand guard shaped like a shallow
> funnel — sits on the shaft just forward of the grip.

Frame: very wide landscape; the length is the point of the image.
Avoid: jousting coronels (blunt crown-shaped tips); spiral paint; tournament
livery colours; heraldic pennons.

## 6. `javelin-throwing-spear` — angon / light throwing spear

> An early medieval European throwing spear of the angon type, c. 500–900, shown
> complete and horizontal. A small narrow barbed iron head with two backward-facing
> barbs, carried on a long thin iron shank about 60 cm long, socketed onto a
> slender ash shaft about 1.5 metres long. Overall much lighter and thinner than a
> thrusting spear.

Frame: wide landscape.
Avoid: Roman pilum proportions with a heavy pyramidal head and wide wooden body;
leaf-shaped blades (that is a thrusting spear).

## 7. `war-bow` — English longbow — **v2, REGENERATE**

**v1 was rejected on review (2026-09-06) for two construction errors:** the pale
sapwood and dark heartwood were **inverted** (sapwood rendered on the belly, next
to the string, instead of on the back), and the stave carried a **carved swelled
grip** at the centre. Both are named explicitly below so they cannot recur.

> An English war bow (longbow), 15th century, photographed complete and vertical,
> lightly strung. A single tapered stave of yew about 1.9 metres long, D-shaped in
> cross-section.
>
> **Wood layering — this is the critical detail.** The bow is cut from one stave so
> that a narrow continuous strip of pale cream sapwood lies along the **BACK** of
> the bow — the outer face, the side that curves AWAY from the string — while the
> darker orange-brown heartwood forms the **BELLY**, the inner face that the string
> sits nearest. Pale wood on the outside of the curve, dark wood on the inside of
> the curve. Never the reverse.
>
> **No handle of any kind.** The stave tapers smoothly and continuously from the
> centre towards both tips; the archer grips the bare middle of the bow. There must
> be no shaped grip, no swelling or bulge at the centre, no leather or cord
> wrapping, no arrow shelf, no cut-out window, no riser and no sight.
>
> Simple pale horn nocks at each tip, grooved for the string. A plain twisted
> linen or hemp string, undyed. No varnish gloss, no laminations or glue lines, no
> maker's marks, stamps or decoration anywhere on the stave.
>
> Photographed as a museum catalogue product shot: the complete bow isolated on a
> plain seamless white background, even soft studio lighting, no shadows beyond a
> faint contact shadow, no scene, no setting, no figure, no hands, no props, no
> text, no watermark, no border. Both tips inside the frame with a small margin —
> nothing cropped at any edge. Natural proportions, no perspective distortion,
> photorealistic, sharp focus throughout.
>
> Historical scope: England, 15th century. Not a modern recurve, flatbow or
> laminated target bow; not Asian, Ottoman or composite; no reflex or deflex limb
> shaping.

Frame: tall portrait, bow vertical.

## 8. `coat-of-plates` — 14th-century plated body defence

> A 14th-century coat of plates, shown complete from the front as it would hang on
> the body. A sleeveless torso defence of coarse linen or leather, inside which
> rectangular iron plates are riveted in horizontal rows — the plates hidden, but
> their round rivet heads showing in regular rows across the outer face. It opens
> at the front and fastens with plain leather straps and simple iron buckles at
> the shoulders and sides.

Frame: portrait, garment shown filled out to its worn shape rather than laid flat.
Avoid: exposed or scale-like overlapping plates on the outside (that is
brigandine or lamellar); decorative studs arranged in patterns; velvet or
patterned fabric; visible modern stitching.

## Verification on receipt

Each image is checked before it goes in: correct period and region, correct form
and proportions, no decoration, no blackening, complete object, plain background,
no watermark. Then it is written with `imageInfo.aiGenerated: true`, a caption
beginning "AI-generated illustration …", and a note recording that no suitably
licensed photograph could be sourced — all three enforced by
`npm run check:images`.
